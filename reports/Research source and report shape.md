# Researchers plan searches while products browse

A working researcher chooses sources by a method, and a current deep-research product chooses them by a search it runs itself. A Cochrane reviewer must search CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase when it is available, plus trials registers, and then check the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews. A graduate literature review usually starts from keywords and a recent review, then follows citations until new searches mostly return work already in hand. A journalist starts from the newsroom's own clips and from named people with direct knowledge, and stops when a claim is corroborated or can be labeled unconfirmed. OpenAI, Gemini, and Perplexity invert that opening move: the person asks, and the product searches the public web, pivots, and writes. Elicit and Consensus search a vendor-built paper index, ranking by meaning, citation count, and recency. The file that comes back is the prose plus links. A methods account, the queries that were run, pages read and left uncited, a slot for disagreement, and a line on what each source was used for stay in the session or in a side file, and they appear inside the download in one documented case: Elicit's systematic-review export, which adds the search strategies and a PRISMA flow diagram.

## Database search comes before the citation chase

The order of looking depends on the kind of work, and citation chasing is a second move in both academic modes because it needs a seed. For a Cochrane intervention review the seed comes after a planned database search. **Mandatory steps are a search of CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase when available, a search of trials registers, and a check of the reference lists of included studies and of relevant systematic reviews.** Specialist databases, grey literature, earlier reviews on the same topic, and contact with experts about unpublished or ongoing studies are highly desirable, and the stated reason is to reduce publication bias and to find as much relevant evidence as possible ([Cochrane MECIR](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)). The database strategy is rewritten as early retrieval shows which terms work, using controlled vocabulary plus free text, and the final strategy is saved so it can be rerun. A date limit is allowed only when the question already has one, for example because the intervention did not exist earlier.

The 2024 TARCiS statement, from a four-round Delphi with 27 methods experts, puts the same order in plain terms. **Evidence syntheses rely on text and keyword searches of databases. Citation searching is supplementary and starts from seed references already found.** Checking the references those seeds cite, backward citation searching, is the common form and is the Cochrane requirement. Looking forward, at later papers that cite the seeds, is used as well. TARCiS says both directions should be seriously considered when the topic is hard to search, including qualitative, mixed-method, public-health, and policy questions whose vocabulary will not sit still, and that results should be deduplicated before screening ([TARCiS statement](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/385/bmj-2023-078384.full.pdf)). Imperial College's guide, updated 6 November 2026, calls databases the main source and citation chaining plus hand searching a safety net. Forward chaining in Scopus and backward chaining through reference lists are done on included studies. If that set is long, the guide allows chaining to be limited to the most cited or the most current studies, and hand searching about two or three journals that already produced many relevant hits is described as enough. A new or niche topic gains more from this extra pass than a well-covered one ([Imperial College London](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)). The Campbell Collaboration's 2024 retrieval guide treats citation chasing the same way, as a complement, and names Citationchaser, SpiderCite, and Paperfetcher as tools for it ([Campbell searching guide](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11386270/)).

A graduate researcher writing an ordinary literature review often reverses the first hour, then lands on the same second move. Oregon State tells most people to start with keywords, then read review articles, search for the author of an important paper, mine the references in a useful chapter, and run a cited-reference search in Web of Science or Google Scholar. Review articles are treated as maps of a sub-field, useful for background, rather than as new results ([Oregon State University](https://guides.library.oregonstate.edu/c.php?g=285832&p=1905947)). Connecticut describes the loop as iterative: plan keywords, search, notice the terms other papers use, search again, and record both commonalities and disagreements. On a heavily studied topic the guide suggests limiting to roughly the last five to ten years. On a new topic it tells the reader to look outside their own discipline before concluding the shelf is empty ([University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)). A 2021 Toronto Metropolitan graduate course puts existing literature reviews first, then the question, then subject databases, then citations backward and forward, and treats the appearance of "the major studies that were recommended" as evidence the search is working ([Toronto Metropolitan University](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/graduatereviews/chapter/when-to-stop-searching/)).

Journalists orient on prior coverage and on people, and the standards reviewed here contain no equivalent of a database-first protocol. An older US Department of State handbook, reflecting newsroom practice and uploaded in 2020, says the organization's own previously published stories are a first stop, and that those clips point to people to interview and to documents such as letters. It defines a primary source as someone with direct experience, an original document, or the journalist as eyewitness, and a secondary source as a later write-up. One source does not supply the whole story, and each source is expected to lead to another ([Handbook of Independent Journalism](https://static.america.gov/uploads/sites/8/2020/06/Handbook-of-Independent-Journalism_Handbook-Series_English_Hi-Res.pdf)). Current standards describe the next pass as checking. Reuters says a journalist or camera on the scene is generally the best source, a named source is preferable to an unnamed one, information should be cross-checked, and the reporter should talk to sources on all sides of a deal, dispute, negotiation, or conflict. Initiative reporting should try to disprove the story as well as prove it ([Reuters](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)). Agence France-Presse's editorial standards of 12 March 2024 tell the reporter to ask how a source knows a fact, whether a figure is a count or an estimate, and whether the source is repeating other media or social-media chatter. Reproducing what was said is not enough ([AFP](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf)).

No methods guide from 2024 to 2026 in this set gives policy analysts, market analysts, or intelligence analysts a reading order of their own. Where university guides describe desk research, they describe the literature-review sequence above. The Oregon State and Connecticut pages are live guides whose exact revision year was not printed on the fetched text.

## Fit, a finding, or a checkable fact decides what stays

What counts as "keep" is three different decisions, and none of them is a pre-set pile of twenty sources. A systematic reviewer keeps a record when it meets eligibility criteria fixed before screening. After duplicates are removed, two reviewers scan titles and abstracts against those criteria. A "maybe" or a record the reviewer cannot judge is kept and sent to full text. **Only a full-text exclusion needs a reported reason.** Virginia Tech, following PRISMA 2020, describes the outcome as a body of included literature plus a list of excluded references with reasons ([Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)). PRISMA item 16 requires the counts that make that decision visible: records identified, duplicates or other records removed before screening, records screened, records excluded, reports sought, reports not retrieved, reports excluded after retrieval with the main reasons, and studies included. Training lists of full-text reasons include the wrong population, intervention, outcome, study design, setting, publication type, or date range, and each excluded report is counted once even if several reasons apply ([PRISMA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71); [UNC Health Sciences Library](https://asklib.hsl.unc.edu/faq/372950)). Australia's NHMRC guide says at least two people should make the final inclusion decision, with a third available to settle disputes, and that full text is judged mainly on the methods section. Studies a reader would expect to see, and which were excluded, go in an excluded-studies table. Multiple reports of the same study are linked at this stage ([NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelinesforguidelines/develop/selecting-studies-and-data-extraction)). Cochrane forbids a casual date or format cut as a way to shrink the pile. Excluding letters because they are letters is generally rejected, because any report of an eligible study can still qualify ([Cochrane MECIR](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)).

A graduate literature review keeps a paper that is on the question and adds a finding, a method, or a disagreement. Connecticut says there is no magic number of sources. An emerging topic yields few papers, and a crowded topic is narrowed with a date window, with the last five to ten years given as the example. While reading, the researcher records which articles are useful and why, including where they disagree ([University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)). Texas A&M University-San Antonio says the amount follows the breadth of the question, the newness of the topic, how many theories are in play, and any limit set by an instructor, a program, or a journal. A hot new topic can produce a large literature quickly. A brand-new topic can produce very little inside one field ([Texas A&M-San Antonio](https://libguides.tamusa.edu/lit_review_support/scope)).

A newsroom keeps named, checkable, first-hand material. Reuters keeps a source that is named when possible, has a track record, holds a position that would let them know, and has a motive the reporter has weighed. It drops reliance on a single anonymous source except in an exceptional case where the source is trusted, has direct knowledge, and a special authorization is followed. It forbids citing "sources" in the plural when there is only one ([Reuters](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)). Associated Press rules, as reprinted by Accountable Journalism and reported by Poynter in 2011, allow anonymous material when it is information rather than opinion or speculation, the information is vital, it cannot be obtained on the record, and the source is reliable and in a position to know. A news manager must approve and must know the source's identity. AP routinely seeks more than one source and holds stories while more sources are sought. A single source is enough only in rare cases where an authoritative figure provides detail that leaves no question of accuracy. That anonymous-source test is older advice that newsrooms still cite ([AP News Values](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles); [Poynter](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2011/ap-no-opinion-or-speculation-from-anonymous-sources/)). AFP, in March 2024, treats anonymity as an exception, used when there is no alternative or for security, and only if the story is important enough. Before granting it, the reporter weighs motive and the chance of manipulation ([AFP](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf)).

Stopping follows the same split. **A systematic reviewer stops when the pre-specified sources have been searched and screened, then reruns that search close to publication.** Cochrane makes it mandatory to rerun or update searches for all relevant sources within 12 months before publication, and says the preferred window is six months. New studies should be incorporated. If they cannot be fully folded in, they are still reported, at minimum under "Studies awaiting classification," or under "Ongoing studies" if the study has not finished ([Cochrane MECIR](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)). A graduate review stops on redundancy. The 2021 Toronto course rejects "if you find 10 articles you can stop" and rejects stopping because the researcher is tired. Its checks are that the strategy is returning known major studies, that all relevant databases have been searched, that citation searching is returning a mix of known and new studies, that adding a term yields nothing new, and that removing a term makes the major studies disappear. The same chapter says "new papers stopped surprising me" is not a justification for ending a systematic search ([Toronto Metropolitan University](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/graduatereviews/chapter/when-to-stop-searching/)). A 2009 methods paper tested capture-mark-recapture as a numerical stop on one osteoporosis review. After four databases the authors had **1,246 known articles** and estimated a horizon of **1,838** (95% CI 1,749 to 1,955), so about 592 still missing. Their known set never fell inside that interval, so the estimate would not have told them to stop, and they said more studies were needed before anyone could set a cutpoint. Cochrane's current standards still require the planned sources ([Kastner et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2655834/)).

For unstructured web search engines the Cochrane Handbook technical supplement (version 6.5) says the searcher must use discretion. Reported Cochrane reviews have limited screening to a pre-specified number of hits, with limits ranging from **100 to 500**, or have stopped ad hoc when results became less relevant. Google Scholar results are capped at **1,000 viewable records**, and screening that full viewable set is described as the more comprehensive practice ([Cochrane Handbook, v6.5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/PDF/v6.5/chapter-4-technical-supplement)). Those integers cap how far down a ranked list a reviewer scrolls. They do not decide how many hits are kept. When the included set is already large, Imperial allows citation chaining on the most cited or most current studies rather than on every included study ([Imperial College London](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)).

Figures that look like a quota live in teaching pages, and those pages label them as guides for students. Federation University suggests **5 to 20 titles** for an undergraduate review depending on level, **20 or more** for honours, **40 or more** for a masters, and **50 or more** for a doctorate, and says to check with the lecturer. It contrasts a hot cancer topic, which can mean hundreds of papers a week for a PhD student, with a cold historical-philosophy topic that has almost nothing recent ([Federation University](https://www.federation.edu.au/students/study-skills/study-resources/assessments/literature-reviews-for-undergraduates/)). The Open University, in a law unit, says **15 to 25 references, with about 20 typical**, is a general range for that teaching context, that the range is not fixed, and that a smaller set read critically can be the better project ([Open University](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=64922&section=3.4)). University of Maryland Libraries says a literature review for a graduate class can have **15 to 20 references**, while a dissertation review can have **100 or more** ([University of Maryland](https://lib.guides.umd.edu/mechanicalengineering/tips-tools/lit-rev)). Texas A&M lists "How many citations do you plan to include?" as a planning question. The page does not treat that plan as a rule that ends the search. No newsroom standard in this set sets a number of sources that must be read before a story is filed. Reuters' rule is accuracy: it is better to be late than wrong, and the reporter should be explicit about what is not known ([Reuters](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)). The Investigative Journalism Manual says that if a second independent source cannot be found, the story should say the point could not be confirmed ([Investigative Journalism Manual](https://www.investigative-manual.org/chapters/pssst-insight-knowledge/1-how-to-identify-good-human-sources/1-1-evaluating-sources/)). Interview-saturation counts are a different method. A 2024 review reports theme saturation around **9 interviews or 4 focus groups**, meaning saturation around **24 interviews or 8 focus groups**, and theoretical saturation around **20 to 30 or more interviews** ([Wutich, Beresford, and Bernard](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/16094069241296206); [Rahimi and khatooni](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11080421/)). Using those figures as a literature-review quota would mix two designs. No study in this set measures how working graduate researchers from 2024 to 2026 actually decide to stop, as distinct from how library guides tell them to decide.

Three awkward sources get three stable treatments. A source that cannot be opened is a retrieval failure, and it is not treated as if it failed the question. PRISMA's flow diagram has a separate box for reports not retrieved. Virginia Tech tells reviewers to try a full-text finder and interlibrary loan, to contact the corresponding author if the full text still lacks the facts needed to judge eligibility, and to report inaccessible full text on the flowchart ([Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)). UNC says to use the library and interlibrary loan before counting a paper as not retrieved, and to keep that count out of the excluded-at-eligibility box ([UNC Health Sciences Library](https://asklib.hsl.unc.edu/faq/372950)). Oregon State tells a student who hits a citation with no campus subscription to request interlibrary loan, and says the article link typically arrives by email in one business day ([Oregon State University](https://guides.library.oregonstate.edu/c.php?g=285832&p=1905947)). APA Style, 7th edition, says a secondary source should be cited sparingly, for instance when the original is out of print, unavailable, or in a language the writer does not read. The preferred action is to find the primary source, read it, and cite it. If that fails, the in-text citation names the original and the secondary, and only the secondary source goes in the reference list ([APA Style](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/secondary-sources)). Cochrane's "Studies awaiting classification" label is a different delay: studies found in a late rerun that are not yet folded into the review. Library guides do not say how long a graduate researcher waits on a loan that never arrives, or what share of requests fail.

A source that disagrees is kept, because conflict is part of the evidence. Cochrane's breadth exists to reduce publication bias, which Imperial defines as publishing studies with positive effects and rejecting studies with zero or negative effects. A conflicting result is a reason to keep looking ([Imperial College London](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)). Disagreement between the two reviewers is a separate problem, settled by discussion or by a third reader, and low agreement in a pilot is treated as a sign the criteria are unclear ([Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)). Connecticut tells the ordinary reviewer to note disagreements in a spreadsheet while reading, as input to the synthesis ([University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)). The Investigative Journalism Manual says a conflicting second source is reported, for example by stating both official accounts, and that details which spoil the intended story are kept if they are relevant. AFP says even a compelling account needs corroboration or rebuttal, because convincing narratives can be half-true or false ([AFP](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf); [Investigative Journalism Manual](https://www.investigative-manual.org/chapters/pssst-insight-knowledge/1-how-to-identify-good-human-sources/1-1-evaluating-sources/)). No source in this set describes a written practice of quietly dropping a conflicting paper because it complicates the write-up. Whether writers follow the written rule was not measured here.

A source that only repeats another is removed as a duplicate, folded into the same study, or replaced by the original, and it is not counted as independent confirmation. Virginia Tech says duplicates are removed before screening, with a human checking the software flag, because automated matching misses some duplicates and can flag a unique record by mistake ([Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)). PRISMA 2020 separates a report, one document, from a study, one investigation. The same article found twice is a duplicate. One trial can have many reports, a protocol and a later outcomes paper among them, and the flow diagram counts both so a report is not hidden inside a study ([PRISMA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71)). APA's instruction is to read and cite the original research rather than the textbook or lecture that quoted it. AFP says an official who is passing on other media or social-network chatter can be repeating hearsay, and the reporter has to ask where the information came from. AP's rules for picking up another news organization's anonymous-source story require a real effort to confirm it with the newsroom's own sources, and if the pickup runs it is attributed to the originating organization ([AP News Values](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)).

## Products search, pivot, and cite far fewer pages than they open

The person does not have to name sources. Each product searches and picks. On OpenAI, deep research can use the public web and files the person uploads, and it can connect apps that support it, including Google Drive or SharePoint, using those apps' read actions only. In Sites, the person can restrict the run to entered websites or domains, or choose to prioritize those sites and still allow a full-web search. ChatGPT can ask clarifying questions, and it proposes a research plan the person can edit before the run. A 10 February 2026 update says deep research can connect to any MCP or app and can restrict web searches to trusted sites ([OpenAI Help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10500283-deep-research-in-chatgpt); [Introducing deep research](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/)). Gemini uses Google Search by default. The person can add Gmail, Drive, uploaded files, and NotebookLM notebooks, and deselecting Google Search limits the run to the other selected sources. After submit, Gemini writes a research plan the person can edit before Start research ([Gemini Apps Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/15719111?hl=en); [Gemini Deep Research overview](https://gemini.google/overview/deep-research/)). If the person does nothing but ask, the product picks from the public web, Google Search in Gemini's case.

Perplexity's consumer Research mode, in the help text retrieved, performs dozens of searches, reads hundreds of sources, and is started from a mode selector. Advanced Deep Research can process uploaded documents and browse more of the web. Those extracts do not describe a site allow-list or a control for naming websites inside Research mode. The full help pages were unavailable, so a control that exists only in the unread portion is unconfirmed. A different surface, Perplexity Computer, is described as making a research plan, finding primary sources across hundreds of sites, using the person's files and apps, and drawing on premium sources such as Statista, PitchBook, and CB Insights. That description comes from a product-page extract, not from the Research mode help article ([What is Research mode?](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/10738684-what-is-research-mode); [Advanced Deep Research](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/13600190-what-s-new-in-advanced-deep-research); [Perplexity Computer](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/products/deep-research)).

Elicit and Consensus invert the web products. The corpus is chosen by the vendor in advance, and the person's levers are the question, filters, uploads, and which papers to keep. **Elicit ranks a corpus of about 138 million papers** from Semantic Scholar, PubMed, and OpenAlex, updated weekly, plus a separate ClinicalTrials.gov corpus. The person can upload documents, connect company subscriptions for more full text, and optionally filter by year, study type, and keywords ([Elicit's source for papers](https://support.elicit.com/en/articles/14758040-elicit-s-source-for-papers); [Elicit paper search](https://elicit.com/solutions/search)). **Consensus searches an index of over 400 million papers** from Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, its own scholarly-web crawl, and publisher full-text agreements. A sources dropdown chooses All or Medical Mode, about 8 million papers and 50,000 clinical guidelines. The person can also search by title, author, or DOI, upload documents, and chat with specific papers ([How Consensus Works](https://help.consensus.app/en/articles/9922673-how-consensus-works); [Consensus Research Database](https://help.consensus.app/en/articles/10055108-consensus-research-database)).

What happens after the first search is a pivot, and following a bibliography is documented for one product. OpenAI says the model was trained to plan a multi-step trajectory, backtrack, and react to information as it arrives, and the system card says training covered searching, clicking, scrolling, and interpreting files. The same card says deep research is not allowed to navigate to or construct arbitrary URLs. That sentence is a prompt-injection control, and it does not publish a citation-following policy ([Introducing deep research](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/); [Deep Research system card](https://cdn.openai.com/deep-research-system-card.pdf)). Gemini's published loop starts with general context and narrows. A March 2025 Workspace post says searches evolve as the model learns, "just like you might change your keywords after reading a few results," and that source choice uses Google's search algorithm to find quality sources from credible sites. The current overview says that at each step the model reasons over what it already has, looks for missing information and discrepancies, and decides its next move. It does not say the next move is to open the references of a page already fetched ([Google Workspace](https://workspace.google.com/blog/ai-and-machine-learning/meet-deep-research-your-new-ai-research-assistant); [Gemini Deep Research overview](https://gemini.google/overview/deep-research/)). Perplexity's retrieved help says Research mode runs dozens of searches and then reads sources, and that Advanced Deep Research cross-references information. It does not say the run opens outbound citations from a page it has already read. Elicit ranks the whole paper database with embedding models by similarity of meaning, also using citation count and recency, then uses language models to re-rank the **top 1,000**. Without full text it uses the title and abstract. Citation count will prefer papers other papers cite. On the pages retrieved, that is not the same as opening those papers' reference lists ([Elicit paper search](https://elicit.com/solutions/search)). Consensus is the exception with a published citation feature. An ordinary search casts a hybrid semantic-plus-keyword net, refines with recency, citation count, and journal impact, and re-ranks the **top 20** with a larger model. Separately, the product can explore citations and related research, and a Citation Graph shows how papers connect. For complex tasks a Research Agent plans searches and chains tools. When full text exists, search can use methods, results, and discussion ([How Consensus Works](https://help.consensus.app/en/articles/9922673-how-consensus-works)). No retrieved OpenAI, Gemini, or Perplexity page says whether a run extracts reference lists from a page it has already opened. A click can still land on a linked page. That is browse-and-pivot, and the vendors do not describe it as a required snowball from a bibliography.

Vendors publish vague ceilings for the web products and tighter ranges for the academic tools. OpenAI's launch post says ChatGPT will find, analyze, and synthesize **hundreds of online sources**. That is not stated as a maximum or a minimum, and the help text says the run reads "many sources," with no number ([Introducing deep research](https://openai.com/index/introducing-deep-research/); [OpenAI Help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10500283-deep-research-in-chatgpt)). Gemini's overview says it can automatically browse **up to hundreds of websites** and can process hundreds of pages in a session, kept in a 1 million token context plus retrieval. Help says a report usually takes 5 to 10 minutes, longer for a complex one. No integer cap was found, including for the two 2026 API agents ([Gemini Deep Research overview](https://gemini.google/overview/deep-research/); [Gemini API](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/deep-research)). Perplexity's Research mode performs **dozens of searches and reads hundreds of sources**, and completes most tasks in under 3 minutes, sometimes about 4 to 5. The Computer page says thousands of retrieval steps across hundreds of sources. Neither extract gives a hard cap. Elicit shows anywhere from the **top 4 to the top 1,000**, depending on plan and workflow, and a search it judges irrelevant can show fewer than 10 ([Improve search results](https://support.elicit.com/en/articles/14758175-improve-search-results)). Consensus ends an ordinary search in a re-rank of the top 20, and Deep Review is an in-depth synthesis of **50 to 100 papers**. Those are ranges for named modes. A usage limit is a different ceiling: as of the 24 April 2025 update on OpenAI's launch post, Plus, Team, Enterprise, and Edu accounts got 25 deep research queries a month, Pro got 250, and Free got 5, after which queries switched to a lightweight version. The help page, current as of 11 November 2026, says the in-product counter shows remaining tasks and that a fixed monthly allowance resets 30 days from first use. It does not, in the text retrieved, restate those April integers, and the lightweight model has no separate source-count documentation in the pages retrieved.

Dated runs cite fewer sources than the word "hundreds," and they often run more searches than they cite. On one shared prompt on 24 March 2025, Sarah Constantin reported **Perplexity using 15 sources, Gemini 38, ChatGPT 33, and Elicit 40**. She wrote that the tools are typically capped at 40. That sentence is her generalization from this comparison. Completion times were 2 to 15 minutes, and none took more than 20. On that medical prompt, Perplexity used popular sites such as WebMD and Medical News Today, Gemini used those plus Healthline, and ChatGPT Deep Research and Elicit used research papers. She does not describe any tool walking a citation chain ([Sarah Constantin](https://sarahconstantin.substack.com/p/ai-deep-research-tools-reviewed)). On one shared business prompt on 8 November 2025, Stephen Smith reported **ChatGPT reviewing 23 sources and running 11 searches in 21 minutes, Gemini pulling from 80 sources, and Perplexity running 145 web searches and using 32 sources**. He also writes that Perplexity claimed "175+ pages" and produced seven ([Stephen Smith](https://www.smithstephen.com/p/i-ran-identical-research-through)). A later Sonar Deep Research API call, logged as 10 May 2026, returned **50 citations in 128.9 seconds** ([perplexity-deep-research README](https://github.com/dzivkovi/perplexity-deep-research/blob/main/README.md)). A comparison in The Conversation, with no date in the available text, reported Gemini searching 206 sources and using 58, Perplexity searching 51 and using 5, and Elicit searching 10 and using 4 ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/estudo-mostra-vantagens-e-limitacoes-do-uso-das-ferramentas-de-pesquisa-profunda-em-trabalhos-academicos-252131)). Constantin's ceiling of 40 does not survive the later Gemini counts of 80 and, in an undated comparison, 122 ([Deep Research Showdown](https://www.neuralmarkets.decisional.com/p/deep-research-showdown)). Sources opened and sources cited are different counts. Vendor "hundreds" language can describe pages read, while a reader counting footnotes will see a smaller number. No retrieved page states a hard maximum of pages opened for one OpenAI, Gemini, or Perplexity consumer run.

No retrieved vendor page describes a filter whose job is to drop a page because it makes the same claim as a page already opened. What is documented is a later synthesis step. Gemini's searches start broad and narrow, and during synthesis the model identifies key themes and inconsistencies and does multiple passes of self-critique. Planning trades comprehensiveness against compute and wait time ([Gemini Deep Research overview](https://gemini.google/overview/deep-research/)). OpenAI's model is trained to backtrack and to synthesize a large number of websites, and the launch post warns that the model has trouble distinguishing authoritative information from rumors. A line in the API cookbook, "If you've already retrieved a file, do not call fetch again for that same file," is an example instruction a developer can send, not a documented ChatGPT deduplication step ([Deep Research API cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/deep_research_api/introduction_to_deep_research_api)). Perplexity's retrieved help says Advanced Deep Research cross-references information, and it does not describe near-duplicate removal or a diversity quota. Elicit's cleanup of overlapping records from Semantic Scholar, PubMed, and OpenAlex eliminates incomplete listings. That is duplicate-record cleanup. Ranking by meaning can put papers that say similar things at the top, and the documented counters are the language-model re-rank, a drop of papers judged irrelevant, and the person's own screening questions and keyword exclusions ([Elicit's source for papers](https://support.elicit.com/en/articles/14758040-elicit-s-source-for-papers)). If it cannot find sufficient relevant evidence, it says so rather than filling the gap from outside the papers. Retracted papers are excluded from its AI analyses. Citation count and journal impact still favor heavily cited work, so the top 20 can cluster on the same well-known literature. The citation graph is the documented way to move to related and later papers ([How Consensus Works](https://help.consensus.app/en/articles/9922673-how-consensus-works)). Elicit's report was "all neurological," which she contrasts with ChatGPT also covering precancerous conditions. ## The download keeps the answer and leaves the trail in the session

A full university research report, in the teaching templates, carries several sections besides the body prose and the reference list, and a short briefing keeps fewer of them. The University of the Sunshine Coast describes a scientific report as IMRaD: **title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, and discussion**, with the conclusion often the last paragraph of the discussion. The abstract is a bit of background, the research question, a summary of the main methods, and a basic conclusion. Results report what was found and stay separate from what the findings mean ([University of the Sunshine Coast](https://libguides.usc.edu.au/scireport/imrad)). Wits lists six sections for a clinical research report: abstract, introduction and literature review, materials and methods, results, discussion, and conclusion. The conclusion summarises the main findings and also states implications, limitations, and future directions ([Wits](https://www.wits.ac.za/media/wits-university/faculties-and-schools/health-sciences/clinical-medicine/documents/Guidelines%20on%20Writing%20a%20Well%20Structured%20Research%20Report.pdf)). A Hull skills guide says a simple report is likely to include an introduction with aims, a methodology, findings or results, a discussion, conclusions and recommendations, and references. A more complex report adds preliminaries (a title page, terms of reference, contents, a list of tables and diagrams, acknowledgements, and a summary) and supplementaries (a bibliography, appendices, and a glossary) ([Hull report guide](https://grimsby.ac.uk/documents/highereducation/skills/Report-Writing-A-Brief-Guide-Hull.pdf)). The overlap is a title, a statement of the question or aim, an account of how the work was done, what was found, what that means, and a source list. A front summary is nearly as common. Hull places the summary with the longer report's preliminaries, so the shortest outline does not require one.

Briefings keep the title, a summary, findings, and a closing judgment, and they compress the methods. Edinburgh's 2016 guide suggests a title, a summary of about 100 words, key points or findings or recommendations, and an introduction, plus some of boxes, cases, tables, graphics, photographs, and quotations, in a piece of generally 2 to 8 sides ([University of Edinburgh](https://edwebcontent.ed.ac.uk/sites/default/files/atoms/files/how_to_write_a_research_briefing_oct2016.pdf)). Simon Fraser's research-brief template, up to 1,500 words plus references and project details, names title, authors, a summary of the problem, a description of the research, research findings, implications, further research, and key messages. Methods inside the description should take two or three sentences, and the findings block can run to 600 words ([SFU STAR Institute](http://www.sfu.ca/content/dam/sfu/starinstitute/Documents/STAR%20Institute%20Real-World%20Output%20Templates%20and%20Guidlines%20Tool-kit%20(1).pdf)). Recommendations, a standalone literature review, quotations, appendices, a glossary, acknowledgements, and a separate limitations or future-research heading are template-dependent. Hull adds recommendations "if you have been asked to." Wits folds the literature review into the introduction and folds limitations and future directions into the conclusion. SFU asks for a few brief quotes only if the work is qualitative. These are teaching templates. They are not a survey of journal articles or of government briefings in 2025 and 2026, and they do not show how often a finished professional report drops the methods heading. None of these guides names a "confidence" heading. Discussion is where the Sunshine Coast guide puts inconsistencies, comparison with other studies, and limitations.

Product reports follow the question, and the vendors do not publish one outline that every downloaded file must use. OpenAI's help page says the finished result is a structured report with citations or source links. **The only parts it names for every completed research view are a table of contents, a sources-used section, and an activity history.** A sample on the product page, "Retail Banking in the United States vs the European Union," uses question-shaped headings, including "Scope, definitions, and data approach" and a five-slide executive briefing outline with a recommendations subsection, because the prompt asked for a comparison and that briefing. The help page does not list those headings as a required schema ([OpenAI Help](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10500283-deep-research-in-chatgpt); [ChatGPT deep research](https://chatgpt.com/features/deep-research/)). Gemini's help tells the user to edit a plan and then open the finished report. Export choices are Share Canvas, Export to Docs, and Copy Contents. It does not name methods, findings, limitations, or recommendations as required headings. A Docs help page says a Deep Research Google Doc includes the findings, citations, and an organized summary of the data found ([Gemini Apps Help](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/15719111?hl=en); [Google Docs Editors Help](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/17127708?hl=en)). Perplexity's February 2025 launch post says the agent writes a clear and comprehensive report that can be exported to a PDF or a document, or turned into a Page. It does not name the sections. The Advanced Deep Research help extract says the report adapts its depth, tone, and format to the work ([Introducing Perplexity Deep Research](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/introducing-perplexity-deep-research); [Advanced Deep Research](https://www.perplexity.ai/help-center/en/articles/13600190-what-s-new-in-advanced-deep-research)). Elicit's help article of 30 September 2026 offers three report templates, General Review, 1-Page Overview, and Research Gap Analysis, plus a fast, balanced, or comprehensive depth. It does not publish the heading list inside each template. Systematic review reports follow a template chosen on the setup page ([Elicit Help](https://support.elicit.com/en/articles/14756862-get-a-research-report-to-generate-in-depth-answers-automatically-in-elicit)).

## Conclusion

A person who wants a file another researcher can rerun is asking for a different object than the one these products download by default. The sections that make a review checkable, a methods account, a reason for every exclusion, a count of reports never opened, and a visible record of disagreement, stay in the session for OpenAI, Gemini, and Perplexity, and in a side CSV for Elicit's lighter report. Elicit's systematic-review export is the workflow that writes the search strategy and the flow of exclusion into the file the person keeps. Question-shaped headings can still carry a scope note and recommendations when the prompt demands them, as the banking sample shows. Left to the question alone, the download behaves as a briefing: useful as an answer, silent as a protocol.

Web products match the graduate researcher's opening hour, a keyword search that narrows as results arrive, and the journalist's cross-check at the point of writing. The systematic reviewer's required second pass, through reference lists, trials registers, grey literature, and experts, is a separate job, and Consensus is the product that publishes citation exploration as its own feature. Even there, an ordinary search stops at a re-ranked twenty. Footnote counts in the dated runs, from the mid-teens to around eighty, with Gemini sometimes higher, sit beside a course bibliography. A dissertation review of a hundred references, or a Cochrane search that ends only after the planned sources are screened and rerun, is a larger job. Site limits, an edited plan, or a review tool that already screens against eligibility are what put the conflicting paper and the unretrieved count into the work. Browsing alone leaves them out.
