# How a working researcher chooses which sources to read

Scope: current desk-research practice for a graduate researcher writing a literature review, a systematic reviewer, and a journalist. "Analyst" as a separate profession did not turn up its own 2024-2026 sourcing sequence; where university research-methods guides describe desk research, they describe the literature-review sequence below. Dates are given so older advice stays marked as older.

## In what order do they look: known authorities, citations, search, recent reviews?

### Takeaway
The order depends on the kind of review. A Cochrane-style systematic reviewer searches bibliographic databases and trials registers first, then checks reference lists and earlier reviews. A graduate researcher doing an ordinary literature review usually starts with keywords and recent review articles, then follows citations out from the useful papers.

### Cited Findings
- Cochrane requires, as mandatory steps, a search of CENTRAL, MEDLINE, and Embase when available (standard C24), trials registers (C27), and the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews (C30). Searching specialist databases (C25), grey literature (C28), and earlier reviews on the same topic (C29), and contacting experts about unpublished or ongoing studies (C31), are highly desirable. The stated reason for this breadth is to reduce publication bias and to find as much relevant evidence as possible. — [Cochrane MECIR, Searching for studies (C24-C38)](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- Building the database strategy is itself iterative: terms are modified based on what early retrieval shows, using controlled vocabulary plus free text, and the final strategy is then documented so it can be rerun (C33, C36). Date limits are allowed only when the eligibility criteria already contain a date limit, for example because the intervention did not exist earlier (C35). — [Cochrane MECIR C32-C36](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- The 2024 TARCiS statement, from a four-round Delphi with 27 methods experts, says evidence syntheses do not primarily rely on citation searching. They rely on text- and keyword-based database searches. Citation searching is a supplementary technique that starts from seed references already found. Checking references cited by those seeds (backward citation searching) is the most common form and is a mandatory Cochrane step. Forward citation searching, looking at later papers that cite the seeds, is also used. TARCiS recommendation 2 says backward and forward citation searching should be seriously considered when the topic is hard to search, including qualitative, mixed-method, public-health, and policy topics with unstable vocabulary. Results should be deduplicated before screening (recommendation 7). — [TARCiS statement, BMJ 2024](https://www.bmj.com/content/bmj/385/bmj-2023-078384.full.pdf)
- Imperial College's systematic-review guide, updated 6 November 2026, calls databases the main source and citation chaining plus hand searching a safety net. Forward chaining in Scopus and backward chaining through reference lists are done on included studies. If the included set is long, the guide says the reviewer may limit chaining to the most cited or most current studies. Hand searching of about two or three journals that already produced many relevant hits is described as enough. A new or niche topic benefits more from this extra searching than a well-covered topic. — [Imperial College London, Supplementary searching](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)
- The Campbell Collaboration's information-retrieval guide (published in the Campbell Systematic Reviews journal, 2024) likewise treats citation chasing as a complement to subject-database searches: check references of included studies, check included studies inside related systematic reviews, and search forward for papers that cite relevant papers. It names Citationchaser, SpiderCite, and Paperfetcher as tools for that step. — [Campbell searching guide, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11386270/)
- For an ordinary literature review, Oregon State University Libraries says most people start with keywords, then use extra moves: read review articles (add "review" as a document type or keyword), search for the author of an important paper, mine references in a useful chapter or article, and run a cited-reference search in Web of Science or Google Scholar. Review articles are described as syntheses of a sub-field, useful for background, rather than as new results. — [Oregon State University, Find Articles](https://guides.library.oregonstate.edu/c.php?g=285832&p=1905947)
- University of Connecticut describes the literature-review search as iterative: plan keywords, search, notice the terms other papers use, search again, organize by concept, and note both commonalities and disagreements while reading. The reader can return to an earlier step at any time. On a heavily studied topic, the guide tells the reader to limit to roughly the last 5 to 10 years. On a new topic, it tells the reader to look outside their own discipline. — [University of Connecticut, Strategies to Find Sources](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)
- Toronto Metropolitan University's graduate review course (copyright 2021) puts the ordinary sequence as: find and examine existing literature reviews, formulate the question, search subject databases, then use citations backward and forward. It treats "the major studies that were recommended" showing up in the results as evidence the search is working. — [Toronto Metropolitan University, When to Stop Searching (2021)](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/graduatereviews/chapter/when-to-stop-searching/)
- Journalists start from what the newsroom already holds, then move to people and original documents. A US Department of State handbook, uploaded in 2020 and reflecting older newsroom practice, says the organization's own previously published stories ("clips") are a first stop, and that those clips point to people to interview and to documents such as letters. It defines a primary source as someone with direct experience, an original document, or the journalist as eyewitness, and a secondary source as a later write-up of that material. It says one source does not supply the whole story, and that each source leads to another. — [Handbook of Independent Journalism (older; America.gov PDF, 2020 upload)](https://static.america.gov/uploads/sites/8/2020/06/Handbook-of-Independent-Journalism_Handbook-Series_English_Hi-Res.pdf)
- Current newsroom standards describe the next pass as checking, not as a database protocol. Reuters says a journalist or camera on the scene is generally the best source, a named source is preferable to an unnamed one, information should be cross-checked, and the reporter should talk to sources on all sides of a deal, dispute, negotiation, or conflict. Reuters also says initiative reporting should try to disprove the story as well as prove it. — [Reuters, Journalistic Standards](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)
- Agence France-Presse's editorial standards dated 12 March 2024 tell the reporter to ask how a source knows a fact, whether it is a count or an estimate, and whether the source is repeating other media or social-media chatter. AFP says it is not enough to reproduce what was said. — [AFP editorial standards, 12 March 2024 (PDF)](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf); the corroboration wording is also quoted by [SPJ, What the Codes Say](https://www.spj.org/what-the-codes-say-code-provisions-by-subject/)

### Inferences
- "Known authorities, then citations, then search, then recent reviews" is not one shared order. Systematic reviewers run a planned database search first and use known papers and reviews afterward as seeds. Graduate desk researchers often do the reverse for orientation: a recent review or a highly relevant paper, then keywords, then the citation network.
- In both academic modes, citation chasing is a second move. It needs a seed. The seed comes from a search or from a review the researcher already trusts.
- Journalists orient on prior coverage and named people with direct knowledge, then test those accounts against documents and against sources on other sides. They do not have an equivalent of PRISMA database-first searching in the standards reviewed here.

### Gaps
- No 2024-2026 methods guide was found that gives policy analysts, market analysts, or intelligence analysts their own reading order distinct from the literature-review sequence or the newsroom sequence.
- The Oregon State and Connecticut pages do not print a "last updated" date on the fetched text. They are live university guides, but the exact revision year was not confirmed.
- The SPJ code page at spj.org returned an error on direct fetch (HTTP 520). The code's "use original sources whenever possible" line is taken from the search excerpt of the SPJ "In Depth" page and the code page, not from a successful full-page fetch.

## What makes them keep a source, and what makes them drop one?

### Takeaway
Systematic reviewers keep a source when it meets eligibility criteria fixed before screening, and they drop it only with a recorded reason after full text. Graduate researchers keep what is on the question and adds a finding, a method, or a disagreement, and they narrow by date when the field is crowded. Journalists keep named, checkable, first-hand material and drop anonymous opinion, unverified repetition, and single unnamed accounts that fail an editor's test.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic-review screening has two stages after duplicates are removed. Two reviewers scan titles and abstracts quickly against pre-set criteria. A "maybe" or "can't tell" record is kept and sent to full text. Only full-text exclusions require a reported reason. Virginia Tech's guide, following PRISMA 2020, lists the outcome as a body of included literature plus a list of excluded references with reasons. — [Virginia Tech, Eligibility Screening](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)
- PRISMA 2020 item 5 requires inclusion and exclusion criteria and a description of how studies were grouped. Item 16 requires counts of records identified, duplicates or other records removed before screening, records screened, records excluded, reports sought, reports not retrieved, reports excluded after retrieval with the main reasons, and studies included. Common full-text exclusion reasons used in library training include wrong population, intervention, outcome, study design, setting, publication type, or date range. Each excluded report is counted once even if several reasons apply. — [PRISMA 2020, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71); [UNC Health Sciences Library, PRISMA flow FAQ](https://asklib.hsl.unc.edu/faq/372950)
- Australia's NHMRC guide says at least two people should make the final inclusion decision, with a third available to settle disputes. Uncertain title-and-abstract records are retrieved. Full text is judged mainly on the methods section. Studies a reader would expect to see, but which were excluded, go in an excluded-studies table. Multiple reports of the same study are linked at this stage. — [NHMRC, Selecting studies and data extraction](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelinesforguidelines/develop/selecting-studies-and-data-extraction)
- Cochrane forbids casual date or format limits as a way to shrink the pile. A date restriction must match a real eligibility limit. Excluding letters by format is generally rejected, because any report of an eligible study may matter (C35). — [Cochrane MECIR C35](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- For ordinary literature reviews, Connecticut says there is no magic number of sources. An emerging topic may yield few papers, and the researcher is told to look in other disciplines before concluding the topic is empty. A crowded topic is narrowed with date limiters, with a suggested window of the last 5 to 10 years. While reading, the researcher records which articles are useful and why, including disagreements. — [University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)
- Texas A&M University-San Antonio says how much to include depends on how broad the question is, how new the topic is, how many theories are in play, and any limit set by an instructor, a program, or a journal. A hot new topic can produce a large literature quickly. A brand-new topic can produce very little inside one field. — [Texas A&M-San Antonio, Determining What to Include/Exclude](https://libguides.tamusa.edu/lit_review_support/scope)
- Reuters keeps a source that is named when possible, has a track record, a position that would let them know, and a motive the reporter has weighed. It drops reliance on a single anonymous source unless the case is exceptional, the source is trusted and has direct knowledge, and a special authorization procedure is followed. It forbids citing "sources" in the plural when there is only one, and forbids quoting someone on the record in a way that contradicts what they said on background. — [Reuters, Journalistic Standards](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)
- Associated Press rules, as reprinted by Accountable Journalism and reported by Poynter in 2011, allow anonymous material only when it is information rather than opinion or speculation, the information is vital, it cannot be obtained on the record, and the source is reliable and in a position to know. A news manager must approve and must know the source's identity. AP "routinely seeks and requires more than one source" and holds stories while more sources are sought. A single source is enough only in rare cases where an authoritative figure provides detail that leaves no question of accuracy. The Poynter piece is from 2011, so this anonymous-source test is older advice that newsrooms still cite. — [AP News Values, via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles); [Poynter, 2011](https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2011/ap-no-opinion-or-speculation-from-anonymous-sources/)
- AFP (12 March 2024) says anonymous sources are an exception, used when there is no alternative or for security, and only if the story is important enough. Before granting anonymity the reporter weighs motive and the chance of manipulation. Quotes must not be altered or clipped so that they misrepresent the statement. — [AFP, 12 March 2024](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Keep" in a systematic review is an eligibility decision made against a protocol, shared by two readers. Relevance at title-and-abstract stage is a low bar: uncertainty keeps the record. The real drop happens at full text, with a reason.
- "Keep" in a graduate literature review is a contribution decision. A paper stays if it changes the map of the question. Date cutoffs are a workload tool on mature topics, and Cochrane explicitly rejects that tool unless the question itself has a start date.
- "Keep" in a newsroom is a verification decision: direct knowledge, a name, a document, or a second independent account. Opinion, repetition, and an unnamed source who will not meet the editor's test are dropped before publication.

### Gaps
- The university guides describe relevance, date, and peer-reviewed status. They do not publish a scored rubric (for example a point system) that working graduate researchers use to accept or reject each paper.
- No current newsroom document in this set states a numerical quality score for documents. The decision is described in rules about identity, motive, and corroboration.

## How do they decide they have read enough?

### Takeaway
Systematic reviewers stop when the pre-specified sources have been searched and screened, then rerun that search close to publication. Graduate researchers stop when further searches and citation chasing mostly return work they already have and the main studies and debates are present. Journalists stop when a claim is corroborated or when they can say plainly that it is not.

### Cited Findings
- Cochrane C37 makes it mandatory to rerun or update searches for all relevant sources within 12 months before publication, and says the preferred window is six months. New studies should be incorporated (C38, highly desirable). If they cannot be fully incorporated, they are still reported, at minimum under "Studies awaiting classification," or under "Ongoing studies" if the study has not finished. — [Cochrane MECIR C37-C38](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- Toronto Metropolitan University (2021) says an ordinary literature review has no firm rule such as "if you find 10 articles you can stop." Its stopping checks are: the strategy is returning known major studies or clearly eligible papers; all relevant databases have been searched; citation searching has been used and is returning a mix of known and new studies; adding a new term yields nothing new; removing a term makes the major studies disappear. The same chapter says a systematic review stops according to its protocol, and that "new papers stopped surprising me" is not a justification for ending a systematic search. — [Toronto Metropolitan University, 2021](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/graduatereviews/chapter/when-to-stop-searching/)
- A 2009 methods paper tested capture-mark-recapture as a stopping rule on one osteoporosis review. After four databases the authors had 1,246 known articles and estimated a horizon of 1,838 (95% CI 1,749 to 1,955), so about 592 articles still missing. They concluded that more studies were needed before anyone could set a cutpoint, and that their own known set never fell inside the horizon's confidence interval, so the estimate would not have told them to stop. This is older work, and Cochrane's current standards still require the planned sources rather than this estimator. — [Kastner et al., BMC Medical Research Methodology, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2655834/)
- For unstructured web search engines, the Cochrane Handbook technical supplement (version 6.5; passages dated through 2024) says the searcher must use discretion. It reports that Cochrane reviews have limited screening to a pre-specified number of hits, with reported limits ranging from 100 to 500, or have stopped ad hoc when results became less relevant (citing Stansfield et al., 2016). It also says Google Scholar results are capped at 1,000 viewable records, and that screening that full viewable set is the more comprehensive practice (citing Haddaway et al., 2015). — [Cochrane Handbook, Chapter 4 technical supplement, v6.5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/PDF/v6.5/chapter-4-technical-supplement)
- Reuters' stopping rule for a story is accuracy rather than a source count: "It's better to be late than wrong." The reporter is told to imagine withstanding a challenge or a denial before publication, and to be explicit about what is not known. — [Reuters](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)
- The Investigative Journalism Manual says that if a second independent source cannot be found, or there is no time, the story should say the point could not be confirmed, and that many unconfirmed statements weaken the story. — [Investigative Journalism Manual, Evaluating sources](https://www.investigative-manual.org/chapters/pssst-insight-knowledge/1-how-to-identify-good-human-sources/1-1-evaluating-sources/)
- Saturation research is about interviews and field data, not about how many papers to read. A 2024 concept analysis describes saturation as context-dependent and used in four ways: theoretical, data, code or thematic, and meaning saturation. A 2024 integrative review of qualitative sample sizes reports theme saturation around 9 interviews or 4 focus groups, meaning saturation around 24 interviews or 8 focus groups, and theoretical saturation around 20 to 30 or more interviews. Those figures are sample-size guidance for qualitative data collection. — [Rahimi and khatooni, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11080421/); [Wutich, Beresford, and Bernard, 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/16094069241296206)

### Inferences
- "Enough" for a systematic reviewer is completion of a planned search plus a late rerun, with anything still unread or unfinished labeled as awaiting classification rather than silently omitted.
- "Enough" for a graduate literature review is redundancy: new queries and citation chains reproduce the set already in hand, and the founding and recent work on the question is in that set. Tiredness and a round number are rejected as stopping rules in the 2021 Toronto course.
- Interview-saturation counts (9, 24, 20-30) are a different decision. Using them as a literature-review quota would mix two methods.

### Gaps
- No empirical study in this set measures how working graduate researchers in 2024-2026 actually decide to stop reading, as opposed to how library guides tell them to decide.
- The Investigative Journalism Manual page does not show a revision date in the search excerpt, so its currency relative to 2026 was not confirmed.

## Do they set a number such as 20 in advance, or does the question decide the count?

### Takeaway
Working researchers do not set an inclusion quota such as 20 sources. The question, the maturity of the field, and the review method decide how many survive. A number is sometimes set in advance only as a student-assignment expectation, or as a cap on how many web-search hits to screen, which is a different decision from how many sources to read and keep.

### Cited Findings
- Connecticut states there is "no magic number." The count follows the topic and the type of review. A new topic yields few sources. A mature topic yields many, and the researcher then limits how far back to look, with 5 to 10 years given as the example window. — [University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)
- Texas A&M-San Antonio says the amount of literature follows the breadth of the question, the newness of the topic, the number of theories, and external limits from an instructor, a program, or a journal. It also lists "How many citations/references do you plan to include?" as a planning question, so some reviewers do write down an expected scale before searching. The page does not treat that plan as a rule that ends the search. — [Texas A&M-San Antonio](https://libguides.tamusa.edu/lit_review_support/scope)
- Course pages do publish ranges, and they label them as guides for students. Federation University: undergraduate review 5-20 titles depending on level, honours 20 or more, masters 40 or more, doctoral 50 or more, and it says to check with the lecturer. It contrasts a "hot" cancer topic, which might mean hundreds of papers a week for a PhD student, with a "cold" historical-philosophy topic that may have almost nothing recent. — [Federation University, Literature reviews for undergraduates](https://www.federation.edu.au/students/study-skills/study-resources/assessments/literature-reviews-for-undergraduates/)
- The Open University, in a law-and-research unit, says there is no straightforward answer, that 15 to 25 references with about 20 typical is a general range for that teaching context, and that the range is not fixed. It says quality and fit to the length of the project matter more than a long list, and that a smaller set can be the better project if those sources are read critically. — [Open University, Introducing research in law and beyond, section 3.4](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=64922&section=3.4)
- University of Maryland Libraries says a literature review for a graduate class might have 15 to 20 references, while a dissertation review may have 100 or more, and that length varies by topic, field, and depth. — [University of Maryland, Literature Reviews](https://lib.guides.umd.edu/mechanicalengineering/tips-tools/lit-rev)
- Toronto Metropolitan (2021) rejects "if you find 10 articles you can stop" and rejects stopping because the researcher is tired. — [Toronto Metropolitan University, 2021](https://pressbooks.library.torontomu.ca/graduatereviews/chapter/when-to-stop-searching/)
- Systematic reviews pre-specify sources, concepts, and eligibility. They do not pre-specify how many studies will qualify. Cochrane's minimum is a list of databases and registers, not a list length (C24, C27, C30). — [Cochrane MECIR](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- The one place a number is set before reading is screening depth on the open web. The Cochrane technical supplement says reported Cochrane reviews have capped search-engine screening at 100 to 500 results, and that Google Scholar cannot show more than 1,000 results for a query. — [Cochrane Handbook technical supplement, v6.5](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/handbook/PDF/v6.5/chapter-4-technical-supplement)
- When the included set is already large, Imperial (updated November 2026) allows the reviewer to run citation chaining only on the most cited or most current included studies, rather than on every included study. — [Imperial College London](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)

### Inferences
- A figure like 20 appears in teaching materials as a typical student bibliography, especially the Open University's "around 20." It is not the stopping rule those same materials give for a real review. Federation's 50-plus for a doctorate and Maryland's 100-plus for a dissertation show the count scaling with the question and the degree.
- Planning a rough bibliography size, as Texas A&M suggests, is a scope conversation. It does not license ignoring a later paper that meets the question.
- A pre-set number of search hits (100, 500, 1,000) limits how far down a ranked web list a reviewer scrolls. It does not decide how many of those hits are kept.

### Gaps
- The Federation, Open University, and Maryland ranges are written for students. This search did not find a survey of how many sources working analysts or graduate researchers actually read in 2024-2026.
- No newsroom standard in this set sets a number of sources that must be read before a story is filed. The rule is corroboration, which can be two accounts or, in rare AP and Reuters cases, one highly authoritative account.

## How do they treat a source they cannot open, a source that disagrees, and a source that only repeats another source?

### Takeaway
A source that cannot be opened is logged as not retrieved and is not treated as if it failed the question. A source that disagrees is kept and reported, because conflict is part of the evidence. A source that only repeats another is removed as a duplicate, folded into the same study, or replaced by the original, and it is not counted as independent confirmation.

### Cited Findings
- Cannot open, systematic reviews. PRISMA 2020's flow diagram has a separate box for "Reports not retrieved." A 2022 methods paper in the Journal of the Medical Library Association says reports can be missing because a journal is not in the local collection or available by interlibrary loan, because authors do not reply, or because links are broken. Those reports go in "Reports not retrieved," whatever the reason. They are not given an eligibility exclusion. — [Rethlefsen and Page, JMLA, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9014944/) (page returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch; this wording is from the search excerpt of that paper). The same split is in the UNC library FAQ: use Find@UNC and interlibrary loan before counting a paper as not retrieved, and do not put that count in the excluded-at-eligibility box. — [UNC Health Sciences Library](https://asklib.hsl.unc.edu/faq/372950)
- Virginia Tech tells reviewers to report inaccessible full text on the PRISMA flowchart, to try EndNote's full-text finder and LibKey Nomad, and to request interlibrary loan. If the full text and supplements still lack the facts needed to judge eligibility, the guide says the best practice is to contact the corresponding author. — [Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)
- Oregon State tells student researchers who hit a citation with no campus subscription to use "Request from interlibrary loan," and says the article link typically arrives by email in one business day. — [Oregon State University](https://guides.library.oregonstate.edu/c.php?g=285832&p=1905947)
- Cochrane uses a different label for a different delay. Studies found in a late rerun that are not fully folded into the review are listed as "Studies awaiting classification" (C37). That is a publication-timing category, not the PRISMA "not retrieved" category. — [Cochrane MECIR C37](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38)
- Cannot open, citation practice. APA Style, 7th edition, page last updated July 2022, says a secondary source should be cited sparingly, for instance when the original is out of print, unavailable, or in a language the writer does not read. The preferred action is to find the primary source, read it, and cite it. If the writer cannot, the in-text citation names the original and the secondary ("as cited in"), and only the secondary source goes in the reference list. — [APA Style, Secondary sources](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/secondary-sources)
- Disagreement, systematic reviews. Cochrane's search standards exist to reduce publication bias, which Imperial defines as the practice of publishing studies with positive effects and rejecting studies with zero or negative effects. The search is built to find those non-positive studies, so a conflicting result is a reason to keep looking, not a reason to drop a study. — [Cochrane MECIR](https://www.cochrane.org/authors/handbooks-and-manuals/mecir-manual/standards-conduct-new-cochrane-intervention-reviews-c1-c75/performing-review-c24-c75/searching-studies-c24-c38); [Imperial College London](https://library-guides.imperial.ac.uk/systematic-review/supplementary_search)
- Disagreement between reviewers, as opposed to disagreement between studies, is handled by a pre-set rule: discussion to consensus, or a third reader as tie-breaker. Virginia Tech says this process is reported under PRISMA items on selection. Low agreement during a pilot is treated as a sign the criteria or the form are unclear. — [Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen); [NHMRC](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelinesforguidelines/develop/selecting-studies-and-data-extraction)
- Disagreement, ordinary reviews. Connecticut tells the reader to note "commonalities and disagreement among the research articles" in a spreadsheet while reading, as input to the synthesis. — [University of Connecticut](https://guides.lib.uconn.edu/literaturereview/strategiesfindsources)
- Disagreement, journalism. The Investigative Journalism Manual says a conflicting second source is reported, for example by stating both official accounts, and that details which spoil the intended story are kept if they are relevant. AFP (2024) says that even a compelling account needs corroboration or rebuttal, because convincing narratives can be half-true or false. Reuters says to talk to all sides and to try to disprove the story. — [Investigative Journalism Manual](https://www.investigative-manual.org/chapters/pssst-insight-knowledge/1-how-to-identify-good-human-sources/1-1-evaluating-sources/); [AFP, 12 March 2024](https://www.afp.com/sites/default/files/afp_ethic_march_2024.pdf); [Reuters](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)
- Repeats, same record. Virginia Tech says duplicates are removed before screening so the same PDF is not read twice. Citation managers and tools such as Covidence flag them, and a human checks the flag because automated matching misses some duplicates and can also flag a unique record by mistake. Duplicates found later, during appraisal, are still recorded. PRISMA's flowchart has a place for that later count. — [Virginia Tech](https://guides.lib.vt.edu/SRMA/screen)
- Repeats, same study in more than one paper. PRISMA 2020 separates a "report" (one document) from a "study" (one investigation). The same journal article found twice is a duplicate. A similar abstract at two conferences is still unique. One trial can have many reports: protocol, analysis plan, baseline paper, outcome papers. The JMLA 2022 paper gives the example of results at three months and at two years: one study, two reports. The flow diagram counts both, so the arithmetic does not hide a report. — [PRISMA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71); [JMLA, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9014944/)
- Repeats, a paper that only restates someone else's finding. APA's instruction is to read and cite the original research rather than the textbook, encyclopedia, or lecture that quoted it. The secondary source is what gets cited only when the original cannot be used. — [APA Style, updated July 2022](https://apastyle.apa.org/style-grammar-guidelines/citations/secondary-sources)
- Repeats, journalism. AFP (2024) says an official who is passing on other media or social-network chatter may be repeating hearsay, and the reporter has to ask where the information came from. AP's rules for picking up another news organization's anonymous-source story require a real effort to get the information on the record or to confirm it with the newsroom's own sources, and the story is held if doubt remains. If the pickup runs, it is attributed to the originating organization and to that organization's description of its sources. — [AFP via SPJ compilation](https://www.spj.org/what-the-codes-say-code-provisions-by-subject/); [AP via Accountable Journalism](https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/news-values-and-principles)
- The older independent-journalism handbook says secondary sources are most useful for confirming what primary sources already provided, and that contradictions are settled by the weight of evidence or by going back to original documents. — [Handbook of Independent Journalism (older)](https://static.america.gov/uploads/sites/8/2020/06/Handbook-of-Independent-Journalism_Handbook-Series_English_Hi-Res.pdf)

### Inferences
- Three different "problem sources" get three different treatments, and the treatments agree across the academic guides. Access failure is a retrieval status. Conflict is evidence. Repetition is not a new study and not a second source.
- Journalists apply the same three treatments in newsroom language: say what you could not confirm, print the contradiction, and do not let a clip or a repeated quote stand in for the original.
- A graduate researcher who cannot open a paper is told to borrow it. Citing the commentary that mentioned it is the fallback, and the citation has to show that the original was not read.

### Gaps
- The JMLA 2022 page could not be fetched (HTTP 403). The "reports not retrieved" definition used here rests on the search excerpt plus the UNC library FAQ, which states the same practice.
- Library guides do not say how long a graduate researcher waits on interlibrary loan before giving up, beyond Oregon State's "typically one business day" for a request that succeeds. They do not say what share of requests fail.
- No source in this set describes a current practice of quietly dropping a conflicting paper because it complicates the write-up. The written rules say the opposite. Whether writers follow those rules was not measured here.
